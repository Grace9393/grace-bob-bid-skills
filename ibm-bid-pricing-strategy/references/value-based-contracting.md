# Value-Based Contracting Principles

Use this reference when a bid involves moving away from PxQ, FTE, rate-card, or other input-based commercial models toward pricing that rewards outcomes, automation, service quality, and long-term value.

## Core Thesis

PxQ and FTE-based models can be useful for estimating cost, but they can misalign incentives when they become the client-facing value model for modern digital services.

Common failure modes:

- Suppliers are rewarded for labour effort rather than delivered outcomes.
- Automation reduces supplier revenue, making innovation commercially irrational.
- The client pays the same even when manual work falls.
- Quality, accuracy, user experience, and compliance become secondary to activity and utilisation.
- Improvements become change requests rather than built-in continuous improvement.
- The relationship becomes transactional, focused on roles, rates, hours, and scope disputes.

Value-based contracting corrects this by aligning commercial success with the outcomes the client needs.

## Foundational Principles

### 1. Outcomes, Not Inputs, Drive Value

Define the client outcomes first. Relevant outcome domains may include:

- Accuracy and quality
- Timeliness and responsiveness
- Statutory or regulatory compliance
- Resilience and availability
- Digital adoption and channel shift
- Automation coverage and reduced manual intervention
- Data quality and auditability
- User experience and accessibility
- Reduced rework, backlog, and avoidable contact
- Total cost of ownership reduction

Payment should reflect delivery against these outcomes, not only the labour used to perform tasks.

### 2. Automation Must Be Commercially Positive

The commercial model should make it rational for IBM to reduce manual work through:

- Outcome payments
- Gain-share
- Automation targets
- Innovation funds
- Multi-year efficiency pricing
- Reduced operating cost with shared benefit
- Continuous improvement obligations

If automation only reduces IBM revenue, the model is misaligned.

### 3. Elasticity Should Come From Technology, Not Just Headcount

Modern digital services often have variable volumes and event-driven peaks. Pricing should support elastic capacity through:

- Automation
- Intelligent routing
- Straight-through processing
- Dynamic workload allocation
- Digital self-service
- Exception-based handling
- Surge thresholds and volume bands

Avoid models that rely only on fixed FTE pools when demand is unpredictable.

### 4. Shared Risk And Shared Reward

Allocate risk deliberately:

- Supplier should own operational performance risks it can control.
- Client retains statutory and policy risk that cannot be transferred.
- Transformation and innovation risks may be shared through gated funding or gain-share.
- Third-party dependencies need explicit assumptions and relief events.

Use outcome payments, service credits, risk/reward bands, and gain-share to align incentives.

### 5. Transparency And Data Quality Are Non-Negotiable

Value-based pricing requires credible measurement. Establish:

- Validated baselines
- Shared dashboards
- Data quality controls
- Audit-ready reporting
- KPI definitions and calculation methods
- Anti-gaming review
- Governance forums that can interpret data in context

Do not tie material payments to immature or unreliable data without a shadow-running period.

### 6. Continuous Improvement Must Be Contractual

The contract should require and fund improvement, not treat every improvement as a new negotiation.

Use:

- Annual automation baselines
- Backlog reduction targets
- Data quality improvement targets
- Accessibility and user experience improvement obligations
- Technology roadmap refresh
- Innovation fund governance
- Periodic KPI recalibration

### 7. Partnership Must Be Incentivised

For complex transformation, the supplier must behave as a strategic partner, not a commodity labour provider.

Commercial mechanisms can reward:

- Transparent reporting
- Proactive risk identification
- Constructive challenge
- Governance participation
- Joint planning
- Innovation backlog ownership
- Responsiveness to emerging issues

Performance-based partnering is useful when behaviour and collaboration materially affect outcomes.

## Internal Costing Vs External Pricing

It is acceptable to use FTEs, rates, offshore mix, and effort estimates internally. Internal economics still need to be understood.

Do not automatically make those inputs the client-facing price basis.

Recommended pattern:

1. Build an internal cost baseline using rate cards and delivery assumptions.
2. Translate the cost baseline into a client-facing pricing model tied to outcomes, service levels, value creation, and risk.
3. Explain how IBM can improve service performance while sharing or reinvesting efficiency gains.
4. Document any residual assumptions where the client still constrains pricing to T&M or rate cards.

## Signs The Model Is Misaligned

Reconsider the model if:

- IBM earns less when automation succeeds.
- The client cannot see whether spend improves outcomes.
- Quality and compliance are only reporting metrics, not commercial levers.
- The model requires a change request for routine optimisation.
- FTE utilisation matters more than error reduction or cycle time.
- The supplier can meet contractual obligations while the service remains inefficient.
- Savings cannot be measured, shared, or reinvested.

## Pricing Narrative Language

Use this positioning in proposals:

```markdown
IBM's pricing model separates the cost of delivering a safe and resilient service from the value mechanisms that incentivise measurable improvement. Rather than rewarding manual effort, the proposed commercial model rewards accuracy, timeliness, digital adoption, automation, compliance, and continuous improvement. This aligns IBM's commercial success with the outcomes the client needs and creates a transparent basis for reinvesting efficiency gains over the contract lifecycle.
```
