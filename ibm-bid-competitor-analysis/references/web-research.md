# Public-Source Competitor Intelligence

Use this reference when the competitor analysis depends on information that could have changed recently or is not present in the bid pack.

## When To Use Web Research

Run public-source research when any of these materially affect the conclusion:

- likely bidder identification is uncertain
- a named competitor's current offer or market position matters
- recent client wins, losses, or sector momentum may shape buyer confidence
- partnerships, acquisitions, or product launches may change rival credibility
- geography, hiring, certifications, or public delivery footprint may influence the procurement
- analyst, press, or framework signals may change how evaluators perceive the field

Do not browse just to bulk-collect generic company descriptions.

## Source Hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order:

1. Official company sources
   - competitor website
   - press releases
   - case studies
   - investor materials
   - careers pages
2. Client or procurement sources
   - incumbent disclosures
   - contract award notices
   - framework supplier lists
   - procurement portals
3. Independent third-party sources
   - credible analyst or industry coverage
   - reputable trade press
   - public filings
4. Secondary aggregation sources
   - only when primary sources are unavailable and the point is low stakes

Never treat anonymous commentary or generic SEO articles as decisive evidence.

## Research Workflow

1. Start with the bid hypothesis, not the search engine.
   Write down what you need to validate: likely rival, sector proof, partnership depth, regional presence, incumbent status, or delivery footprint.
2. Search narrowly.
   Use targeted queries combining competitor name, client name, sector, platform, geography, framework, and recency.
3. Capture only the facts that affect the bid.
   Ignore broad marketing copy unless it directly shapes evaluator perception.
4. Cross-check material claims.
   For important assertions, seek at least two independent signals or one strong primary source.
5. Separate fact from inference.
   Example: "Competitor launched offering X" is a fact if sourced; "this will resonate with the buyer" is your inference.
6. Fold the result back into the bid analysis.
   Explain why the sourced fact changes the competitor view or IBM response.

## Recommended Search Themes

Search for evidence across these themes:

1. Sector relevance
2. Comparable case studies
3. Platform and alliance strength
4. Geography and local delivery presence
5. Public-sector, regulated-sector, or framework footprint
6. Managed-service and transition credentials
7. AI, automation, or innovation claims
8. Hiring patterns and capability investment
9. Recent acquisitions or strategic pivots
10. Client-specific or incumbent signals

## Attribution Rules

For sourced competitor points, cite the source category inline:

- `Official source`
- `Procurement source`
- `Third-party source`

If a point is built from multiple sources, say so briefly.

Use wording like:

- `Evidenced: Official source reports...`
- `Evidenced: Procurement source shows...`
- `Inferred: Based on two recent public wins in this sector...`

## Guardrails

- Do not present competitor marketing claims as proven delivery fact unless corroborated.
- Do not infer confidential commercial posture from public branding alone.
- Do not over-weight a single press release.
- Do not assume that public partnerships equal deep delivery capability.
- Do not claim a competitor is bidding unless there is evidence or the statement is clearly framed as a hypothesis.

## Output Additions

When web research is used, add two short elements to the response:

1. `Public-Source Signals`
   List only the current sourced facts that materially changed the analysis.
2. `Source Confidence Notes`
   State where the evidence is strong, mixed, or thin.
