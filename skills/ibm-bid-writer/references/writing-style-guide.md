# Writing Style Guide

Use this guide for full writing standards when drafting tender responses.

## Writing Modes

### Default: Formal Scored-Response Mode

Use this for:
1. Tender responses
2. ITT and RFP answers
3. Public-sector procurement submissions
4. Any evaluator-facing bid content

Target style:
1. Formal and enterprise-oriented
2. Written for scorers, not casual readers
3. Dense with evidence, mechanisms, and buyer value
4. Confident without becoming vague or theatrical

### Optional: Conversational Drafting Mode

Use this only for:
1. Internal strawman drafts
2. Workshop drafts
3. SME input consolidation
4. Early shaping before conversion into scored bid prose

Target style:
1. Clearer and looser internal working tone
2. Still specific and commercially useful
3. Not suitable as the final default tone for scored procurement responses

If the user does not specify a mode, use **Formal Scored-Response Mode**.

## Core Principles

1. Build a clear narrative that demonstrates understanding of client context, route to outcomes, and IBM differentiation.
2. Use active voice with accountable actors.
3. Keep language plain, precise, and specific.
4. Connect technical points to business outcomes using the "So What?" test.
5. Use British English spelling consistently.
6. Build trust by naming the mechanisms, controls, forums, or assets that make delivery credible.
7. Write for scorers as well as readers: make it obvious where the requirement has been answered and why IBM is believable.

## Active Voice

Prefer:
- "Our engineers designed the platform for your service users."

Avoid:
- "Design decisions were made based on user specifications."

Avoid over-commitment terms such as "ensures" or "guarantees" when the evidence cannot support certainty.

## Concision and Clarity

1. State the point first, then add supporting detail.
2. Keep paragraphs short and coherent.
3. Prefer concrete metrics and examples over generic claims.
4. Keep terminology consistent through the response.
5. Use "while" rather than "whilst".
6. Do not mistake brevity for thinness. In high-value bids, specific detail often scores better than stripped-down simplicity.

## Quantified Benefits

Every meaningful benefit claim should include:
1. Baseline
2. Improvement
3. Timeframe
4. Financial or operational effect where available

Examples:
- Poor: "Reduces response time by 40%."
- Good: "Reduces response time by 40% (from 10 minutes to 6 minutes) versus manual processing."
- Better: "Reduces response time by 40% (from 10 minutes to 6 minutes), saving about GBP500,000 annually in staffing cost."

## Response Strategy

1. Mirror the client's terminology and sequence of sub-questions.
2. Mirror client numbering exactly where the procurement question uses numbered parts.
3. Address each required part explicitly.
4. Tailor value statements to technical, finance, operations, and compliance audiences.
5. Demonstrate innovation through practical delivery methods, not generic language.
6. Use one document-level confidence section where it improves evaluator orientation; do not force a fresh confidence opener into every subsection.
7. Pair ambition with control: if you propose transformation, show the governance, standards, and assurance that make it safe.
8. Use named operating mechanisms where possible, such as delivery lifecycle, transition model, dashboard, governance board, charter, workbench, or accelerator.
9. Draft major sections around promise -> mechanism -> proof.
10. Use flowing prose paragraphs as the default format for final scored answers.
11. Use tables to reduce evaluator effort when mapping coverage, responsibilities, commitments, profile fit, or rationale.
12. Bullets are banned in the written response unless the question explicitly enumerates discrete parallel items or the user requests bullets. If bullets are used, record the justification in the pre-draft plan.
13. Make the response feel safe to buy by showing how IBM reduces oversight burden, dependency risk, transition risk, or compliance risk.

## Evidence Standards

Examples should be:
1. Relevant to sector and challenge
2. Detailed enough to prove scale and outcomes
3. Numerous enough to show repeatable delivery capability

At least one strong example should include:
1. Industry or sector
2. Problem baseline
3. Measured outcome
4. Timeframe
5. Financial impact if known

For partner references:
1. Explain why the partner is needed.
2. Explain what capability they contribute.
3. Explain what risk they mitigate.

Evidence should usually be layered in this order:
1. Account-specific or incumbent evidence
2. Named team or leader credibility
3. Relevant public sector or NHS precedent
4. Broader IBM capability proof

## Factual Accuracy

1. Do not invent numbers, case studies, or technical facts.
2. Mark unsupported content with `{{++REPLACE: text}}`.
3. Replace unsupported content with verified source-backed statements.

## Precision Requirements

Cover where relevant:
1. Risks and mitigations
2. Delivery prerequisites and dependencies
3. Governance and approval dependencies
4. Deployment time, payback period, and TCO factors
5. Scalability and future-proofing considerations

Example:
- Poor: "20% of the team will be apprentices."
- Good: "The team includes five apprentices, representing 20% of total headcount."

## Anti-AI Style Rules

### Punctuation Rules

Do not use em dashes in tender response prose. They are a common AI-writing tell and can make formal bid prose feel synthetic.

Use one of these replacements:
1. Comma, when the second clause is a light aside.
2. Colon, when the second clause explains or names the first.
3. Semicolon, when two complete clauses are tightly linked.
4. Parentheses, when the aside is useful but secondary.
5. A new sentence, when the sentence is already long.

Avoid en dashes as sentence punctuation for the same reason. Use a simple hyphen only for compound modifiers or standard terms, such as `two-week cadence`, `risk-based review`, or `standards-by-design method`.

### Avoid These Terms

Avoid by default:
- delve
- leverage (as a verb, unless the sentence is unusually concrete)
- nuanced
- multifaceted
- plethora

Avoid generic transitions and filler:
- "It's worth noting"
- "It's important to remember"
- "In today's digital landscape"
- "Furthermore"
- "Moreover"
- "Additionally"
- "At the end of the day"

Avoid generic jargon:
- streamline
- optimise
- revolutionary
- game-changing
- cutting-edge
- state-of-the-art
- seamlessly
- best practices
- end-to-end solutions
- synergy
- next-generation

Avoid overused adjectives:
- dynamic
- strategic
- holistic
- significant
- substantial

Avoid AI helper phrasing:
- "Here's what you need to know"
- "Let me break this down for you"
- "I hope this helps"
- "In conclusion, it's clear that"

### Prefer Simpler Language

Use:
- use (not utilise)
- help (not facilitate)
- show (not demonstrate)
- buy (not purchase)
- before (not prior to)
- to (not in order to)

### Sound Human

1. Vary sentence length naturally.
2. In formal scored-response mode, avoid contractions unless they appear in quoted source material or client language.
3. Be specific.
4. Keep tone direct, controlled, and evaluator-facing.
5. Replace weak adjectives with mechanisms, numbers, named roles, named controls, or proof.
6. If a word such as `robust`, `comprehensive`, `innovative`, `effective`, `efficient`, or `paramount` is the most natural fit, only keep it when the next clause explains exactly why.

## Confidence-Led Bid Tone

High-scoring bids often need a stronger tone than ordinary business writing. Aim for:

1. Controlled confidence, not chest-beating
2. Specific authority, not vague polish
3. Delivery readiness, not abstract aspiration
4. Persuasion through evidence, not adjective stacking
5. Certainty first, transformation second
6. Collaboration after authority is established

Good:
- "Our proposed team will deliver the service through a two-week sprint cadence, named governance forums, and embedded SRE ownership, reducing handover delays and giving NHSE visibility of product and operational health."

Weak:
- "Our robust and innovative approach will ensure efficient delivery and seamless collaboration."

In formal scored-response mode, prefer evaluator-facing enterprise prose over conversational phrasing.
In conversational drafting mode, you can loosen the tone, but keep the underlying structure, evidence, and mechanisms intact so the draft can be converted into scored-response prose later.

## Format Selection

Use flowing prose paragraphs as the default for final scored responses. Do not let the answer collapse into bullet-heavy briefing notes.

Use:
1. Prose-led sections when the evaluator needs explanation, logic, or narrative flow.
2. Bold-labelled mechanism blocks when the evaluator needs to understand how the operating model works.
3. Tables when the evaluator is checking mappings, boundaries, commitments, ownership, timings, or rationale.
4. Bullets only when the question explicitly enumerates discrete parallel items or the user requests bullets.

For final scored responses, bullets are banned for narrative or method content. If a bullet list explains a method, rationale, governance model, assurance approach, or delivery sequence, convert it into paragraphs or bold-labelled prose unless the tender explicitly asks for a list. When in doubt, write prose.

## Repetition And Memory Anchors

Use disciplined repetition when it improves evaluator recall:

1. Repeat the central bid story across answers.
2. Reuse a small set of named methods, governance artefacts, and anchor phrases where they are genuinely core to the solution.
3. Repeat only what strengthens coherence. Do not recycle slogans that are not evidenced in the answer.

## Human-Centred Variants

For social value, workforce, or community responses:

1. Use slightly more human language.
2. Keep the same structural discipline around commitments, activities, owners, metrics, and reporting.
3. Link social value to delivery reality rather than treating it as a detached statement of intent.
